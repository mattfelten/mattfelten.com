Nesting bench

Sub-items, one level deep. A row is either top-level or a child, never a grandchild. Drag rows around, tick parents, and collapse them to see what each rule costs.

Does this list allow sub-items at all?

Nesting is a capability the component can be told not to offer, not a fact about it. Switch it off and the same component withdraws one gesture and one affordance: no twisties, no dropping onto a row, and the insertion line never insets, because there is nothing to inset to. Only the line appears. Switching it off flattens the list for real rather than just hiding the indents, because a consumer that does not offer sub-items never had nested rows. Ticking a parent, and ticking its children

Settled: they do not affect each other. These are four different components, not four settings, and the multiplayer case decides it. This list has other people's rows in it, so cascading means one tick silently writes to an item somebody else wrote and is watching, and rolling up means a checkbox flips with nobody touching it. A tick changes the row you ticked. The other three are kept here so the trade can be felt: both directions is the only one where a parent's tick is never a lie, and the only one where unticking one child silently unticks the parent.

How dragging places a row

The first one is two gestures rather than one setting. Between rows you get an insertion line, and its indent is read off the gap: after a child means sibling of that child, under a parent means inside it. Over the body of a row there is no line at all, the row lights up, and letting go drops the item inside, the way a file goes into a folder. Nothing depends on a sideways nudge, which is a fine-motor gesture worth not requiring.

What the drop indicator says

The flat list settled on a plain line, and it cannot survive nesting on its own: the same line at the same place would mean two different landings. The other half of the answer is that the line disappears completely when you are dropping into a row, so the list never offers a slot and a container at once.

Move a row, or change its level, and the write shows up here.

Why one level and not many. One level keeps every problem worth having: the index stops being flat, dragging has to carry two things at once, and a parent's checkbox has to mean something. Going deeper adds recursion and a much heavier keyboard contract without adding a new question. A checklist that needs grandchildren is a document, and should be one.

The index is now scoped to a parent. A position only means anything next to its siblings, so moving between levels is not the same operation as moving within one: the row leaves one sequence and joins another.
